01 · Fleet operations

From a pile of parking tickets to an approved payment.

More than 200 paper citations a month, arriving in different formats. Paying them was only part of the problem: who had the vehicle when the citation was issued?

The challenge

A vehicle record alone wasn’t enough. The citation needed to be connected to the person assigned to that vehicle at the relevant time, then routed through notifications, approvals, and payment processing.

What I built

A workflow combining OCR, vehicle lookup, assignment cross-referencing, notifications, payment-system integration, and an approval gateway. The value was in connecting the whole process—not just reading a piece of paper.

Illustrative workflow · not a client screenshot

  1. CaptureScan incoming citations and extract the relevant details.
  2. AttributeLook up the vehicle and cross-reference who had it at the time.
  3. Notify & reviewBring the citation and attribution together for review.
  4. Approve & payUse an approval gateway before the payment-system handoff.

Where the value comes from

Less repeated handling between paper intake, vehicle records, and payment processing. A clearer connection between the citation, the responsible person, and the approval decision.

What I’d check in your workflow

Are assignment histories reliable? What happens when OCR is uncertain or an assignment is ambiguous? Who has authority to approve payment? Those questions matter as much as the extraction technology.

The broader pattern: connect incoming paperwork to the records, people, and decisions needed to act on it.

02 · Field services

Move the quality check closer to the person doing the work.

Technicians report job descriptions and timecodes. Checking those submissions can take substantial effort—especially when the organization already has a history of approved combinations.

The challenge

How do you use that history to identify potential coding problems before submission, instead of relying entirely on downstream quality checks?

What I built

A pre-submission fuzzy-matching check comparing a technician’s description against historical descriptions and approved timecode combinations. It used similarity to surface likely matches while the technician was still completing the report.

Illustrative workflow

  1. DescribeThe technician enters job details and timecodes.
  2. CompareMatch against previously approved work.
  3. ReviewConsider similar descriptions and coding combinations.
  4. SubmitAddress possible inconsistencies before submission.

Where the value comes from

Put relevant prior work in front of the technician at the point of entry. Create an opportunity to catch mismatches before a separate QC cycle and its follow-up conversations.

What I’d check in your workflow

How trustworthy is the historical data? How often do new job types appear? A similar description is a useful clue—not proof that a timecode is correct. Unfamiliar work still needs judgment.

The broader pattern: use accepted prior work to help people make better decisions before mistakes travel downstream.

03 · Payment operations

Look across the payment flow—not just at one transaction.

A payment processor handling customer invoices and vendor payments across up to five currencies needed to minimize dollars lost to bank transaction fees.

The challenge

Customer-side and vendor-side transactions have to be considered together. A convenient choice for one payment may not be the most cost-effective choice across the operation.

What I built

Software supporting multi-currency transaction processing on both sides of that flow, with minimizing bank transaction fees as the objective. This was a payment-operations problem—not a reason to add AI for its own sake.

Conceptual view · not a proprietary algorithm

  1. Customer sideUnderstand invoices and their currencies.
  2. Vendor sideUnderstand payment obligations and their currencies.
  3. Cost objectiveConsider bank transaction fees across the flow.
  4. ProcessingSupport the chosen transaction approach in software.

Where the value comes from

Make transaction costs part of the processing decision instead of treating each leg in isolation. The objective is a more cost-conscious workflow, not a claim of guaranteed savings.

What I’d clarify for a new project

Payment deadlines, available balances, bank fee schedules, exchange costs, and approval rules define the feasible choices. The right approach depends on those constraints; it isn’t a universal FX strategy.

The broader pattern: turn a complicated operational decision into an explicit, repeatable process.
